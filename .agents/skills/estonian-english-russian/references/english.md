## Varieties and register

- Identify or preserve the requested variety: US, UK, Canadian, Australian, international, or another established variety.
- Keep spelling, punctuation, vocabulary, date style, and collective-noun agreement internally consistent.
- Distinguish formal, neutral, conversational, technical, academic, and legal registers.
- Do not label a regional or informal form “wrong” when it is valid in context.

## Grammar

### Nouns, determiners, and articles

- English distinguishes count and mass uses. The same noun may change category by meaning.
- Use `a/an` for one nonspecific singular count noun and `the` when the referent is identifiable in context; use zero article in established generic, plural, mass, institutional, and name patterns.
- Choose `a/an` by sound, not spelling.
- Do not add an article solely because Estonian or Russian lacks one; determine definiteness and countability from meaning.
- Maintain agreement with the grammatical head of a noun phrase while respecting established notional-agreement patterns.

### Verbs, tense, aspect, and modality

- Distinguish time from tense. English uses tense, progressive aspect, perfect aspect, modal verbs, adverbials, and context together.
- Use the progressive for an internally ongoing, temporary, developing, or framed event where idiomatic. Many stative verbs resist it in their stative senses.
- Use the perfect to relate a prior situation to a reference time. Do not select it solely because another language uses a past form.
- Preserve modal strength: possibility, permission, ability, expectation, advice, obligation, inference, and counterfactuality are not interchangeable.
- Use sequence of tense and reported speech according to viewpoint and current relevance, not as blind backshifting.
- Keep phrasal-verb particles and complement patterns with their verbs.

### Clauses and information structure

- Default declarative order is subject–verb–object, with inversion in defined question, conditional, negative-fronting, and stylistic constructions.
- Use dummy `it` or existential `there` when English grammar requires a subject.
- Place modifiers where their attachment is clear.
- Use restrictive and nonrestrictive relative clauses deliberately; punctuation may change meaning.
- Avoid comma splices in edited prose unless consciously used for effect.
- Maintain parallel structure in lists and coordinated phrases.

### Pronouns

- Use singular `they` naturally when gender is unknown, irrelevant, nonbinary, or specified that way.
- Ensure pronoun reference is unambiguous.
- Choose subjective, objective, possessive, and reflexive forms by syntactic role, while recognizing established informal variation.

## Punctuation and spelling

- Apply the selected regional standard consistently: `color/colour`, `organize/organise` where accepted, and related forms.
- In US prose, periods and commas conventionally fall inside closing quotation marks; logical and publication-specific systems differ elsewhere.
- Use the serial comma according to the requested style, but always use punctuation needed to prevent ambiguity.
- Use an en dash for ranges and relationships, an em dash for interruption or parenthesis when the style allows, and a hyphen for many compound modifiers.
- Avoid apostrophes in ordinary plurals. Use them for possession and omitted characters.
- Capitalize proper nouns and defined titles according to context; avoid unnecessary title case in running prose.

## Style and usage

- Prefer concrete verbs and direct syntax, but preserve a formal or cautious register where required.
- Remove redundancy only when it adds no rhetorical or semantic value.
- Prefer idiomatic collocations over dictionary-equivalent word substitution.
- Treat prescriptive shibboleths cautiously. Sentence-final prepositions, split infinitives, singular `they`, and sentence-initial conjunctions can be standard English.
- Distinguish clarity edits from house-style preferences.
- In plain language, lead with the action or conclusion and keep conditions close to what they modify.

## Pronunciation

- Specify the accent or use a broad transcription when pronunciation varies.
- Use IPA when precision is requested; otherwise give a readable approximation and stress.
- Do not imply one prestige accent is the only correct pronunciation.
- Note weak forms, reduced vowels, linking, and stress placement when they affect comprehension.

## Frequent learner problems

- Omitting or overusing articles.
- Mixing simple, progressive, and perfect forms.
- Using a present form after expressions that require an infinitive or gerund, or vice versa.
- Translating Estonian or Russian cases into the wrong English preposition.
- Omitting the grammatical subject.
- Misplacing adverbs or long modifiers.
- Using false cognates or unnatural noun strings.
- Confusing `do` and `make`, or other collocation families.
- Applying spelling and punctuation from more than one regional standard in the same text.
