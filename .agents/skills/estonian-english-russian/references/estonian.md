## Language profile

Estonian is a Finnic language. It has no grammatical gender and normally no articles. Grammatical relations are expressed heavily through case, verb forms, particles, and context. Standard written Estonian is based mainly on northern varieties, while regional languages and dialects remain valid in their own contexts.

## Orthography and pronunciation

- Use the Latin alphabet with `õ`, `ä`, `ö`, and `ü`. The letters `š` and `ž` occur mainly in loans and names.
- Treat `õ` as a distinct vowel, not a spelling variant of `o`.
- Estonian spelling is broadly phonemic, but the three-way quantity contrast is not fully represented in ordinary spelling.
- Primary stress normally falls on the first syllable, though recent loans and proper names may differ.
- Consonant and vowel length can distinguish words. Do not infer pronunciation solely from doubled letters when quantity is the issue.
- Use a hyphen in compounds, abbreviations, or mixed forms according to current orthographic practice; do not transfer English open-compound habits automatically.
- Capitalize sentence starts and proper names. Languages, nationalities, weekdays, months, titles, and ordinary institutional descriptors are generally lowercase unless part of an official name.
- Prefer Estonian quotation marks `„…“` in formal Estonian typography when the environment supports them.

## Nominals

### Number and cases

Estonian has singular and plural and a productive case system. The 14 commonly listed cases are:

| Case | Typical role |
|---|---|
| nominative | citation form, subject |
| genitive | possessor, base for many singular forms |
| partitive | partial or unbounded object, quantity, negation |
| illative | movement into |
| inessive | location in |
| elative | movement from inside |
| allative | movement onto or toward |
| adessive | location on/at; possessor construction |
| ablative | movement from a surface or person |
| translative | change into, role or state |
| terminative | endpoint, “up to” |
| essive | state or role, “as” |
| abessive | absence, “without” |
| comitative | accompaniment, “with” |

Do not generate inflected forms by simply attaching endings to the nominative. Stem alternation, gradation, and lexical inflection classes matter. Consult a reliable morphological dictionary when exact forms are uncertain.

### Total and partial objects

Object case depends on boundedness, completion, polarity, quantity, and construction.

- Use a total object when the event is construed as complete and the object as bounded.
- Use the partitive for ongoing, incomplete, unbounded, quantitatively partial, or negated objects.
- Negation normally requires the partitive object.
- Imperatives, impersonal constructions, and some infinitival constructions affect the surface form of a total object.

Explain this as an aspect-like meaning contrast, not as a one-to-one replacement for English articles or Russian verbal aspect.

### Possession

Estonian commonly expresses possession with an adessive possessor plus `olema`: literally “at someone is something.” Do not force an English `have` structure into Estonian.

### Adjectives and pronouns

- Attributive adjectives usually agree with nouns in number and case, with limited behavior in the last four cases.
- There is no grammatical gender in third-person singular pronouns: `tema/ta` can correspond to English `he`, `she`, or singular `they`.
- Choose long and short pronoun forms according to emphasis and rhythm, not by mechanical substitution.

## Verbs

- Verbs agree with person and number in the present and simple past, except in impersonal forms.
- Core moods include indicative, conditional, imperative, quotative, and jussive.
- Estonian distinguishes affirmative and negative paradigms; negation is not formed by merely inserting one invariant word into an otherwise unchanged affirmative clause.
- The main infinitival forms are traditionally called the `-ma` infinitive and `-da` infinitive. Their complements and constructions differ; learn the governing verb or construction.
- Perfect forms use `olema` plus a participle.
- The impersonal voice presents an action without naming a specific agent. Do not equate it mechanically with every English passive or Russian indefinite-personal sentence.
- Particle verbs and case government are lexical. Preserve the conventional particle and complement form.

## Clause structure

- Basic order is often subject–verb–object, but information structure permits substantial variation.
- Place given or topical information earlier and new or focused information later unless emphasis requires another order.
- The finite verb often occupies an early clause position, but Estonian is not governed by a rigid universal V2 rule.
- Use `kas` for neutral yes/no questions when appropriate; spoken and contextual questions may use other patterns.
- Place negation and clitics idiomatically. Small particles such as `ju`, `ka`, `küll`, `siis`, and `-gi/-ki` carry discourse meaning and should not be translated word for word.
- A comma generally separates subordinate clauses, but punctuation must follow Estonian rather than English conventions.

## Usage and style

- Prefer native, established terminology when it is precise; do not purge useful international vocabulary mechanically.
- Build compounds according to Estonian conventions. English noun strings often need a compound, genitive modifier, adjective, or clause.
- Avoid excessive nominalization and long chains of genitives when a finite clause is clearer.
- Use formal plural address only when audience and convention warrant it. Capitalized pronouns of address are a stylistic politeness choice, not a universal requirement.
- Render dates, decimals, quotation marks, spaces, and abbreviations according to the target publication’s Estonian style.
- Preserve regional Estonian or dialect only when requested or present as meaningful voice.

## Frequent learner problems

- Confusing total and partitive objects.
- Guessing case endings from the nominative without checking stem forms.
- Using English word order when Estonian information structure calls for another order.
- Translating English articles as demonstratives unnecessarily.
- Overusing personal pronouns where verb forms or context make them redundant.
- Mixing `-ma` and `-da` infinitive constructions.
- Writing English-style open compounds.
- Treating `tema/ta` as encoding gender.
- Choosing a literal preposition equivalent instead of the governed Estonian case.
