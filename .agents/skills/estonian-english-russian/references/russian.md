## Language profile

Russian is an East Slavic language written primarily in Cyrillic. It has grammatical gender, six core cases, rich verbal aspect, relatively flexible word order, and a strong distinction between informal singular and formal or plural address.

## Script, spelling, stress, and pronunciation

- Use the modern Russian Cyrillic alphabet, including `ё` where ambiguity, learning, pronunciation, or a requested style calls for it.
- Ordinary publishing often prints `е` for `ё`; do not infer that the words are always pronounced identically.
- Lexical stress is mobile and usually unmarked. Mark it in pronunciation guidance and beginner materials, but not in ordinary prose unless requested.
- Vowel reduction and consonant devoicing or voicing affect pronunciation but usually not spelling.
- `ь` and `ъ` are orthographic signs, not independent vowel sounds.
- Apply spelling rules after velars and sibilants rather than spelling solely from sound.
- Use Russian quotation marks `«…»`, with `„…“` often nested inside.
- Capitalize proper names, but write language names, nationalities, weekdays, months, and ordinary titles lowercase unless another rule applies.

## Nominals

### Gender, number, and animacy

- Nouns are masculine, feminine, or neuter; agreement appears on adjectives, many pronouns, past-tense verbs, and short-form predicates.
- Natural gender and grammatical gender can interact. Follow the referent, lexical convention, and requested inclusive style without inventing nonstandard forms silently.
- Animacy affects accusative forms in parts of the masculine and plural paradigms.
- Indeclinable nouns retain one surface form but still have gender and syntactic case.

### Cases

| Case | Typical role |
|---|---|
| nominative | subject, citation form |
| genitive | possession, absence, quantity, many prepositions |
| dative | recipient, experiencer, obligation constructions |
| accusative | direct object, destination with some prepositions |
| instrumental | means, accompaniment, role, predicate complement |
| prepositional | topic or location after specified prepositions |

Case choice is governed by meaning, preposition, verb, adjective, numeral, and construction. Do not translate English prepositions or Estonian cases one to one.

### Numerals and quantity

- Numerals trigger distinctive case and agreement patterns.
- After forms ending in one, two to four, and five or more, morphology differs, subject to teen exceptions and compound-numeral rules.
- Approximation, collectives, dates, time expressions, and measure phrases have additional conventions.
- Verify full inflection of complex numerals in formal text.

## Verbs

### Aspect

- Most lexical verbs participate in imperfective/perfective relationships, but pairs are not always semantically exact.
- Use imperfective for process, duration, repetition, background, attempt, or the general fact of an action when appropriate.
- Use perfective for a bounded whole, result, or single completed transition when appropriate.
- Aspect interacts with tense, negation, imperative force, infinitival complements, and discourse. Do not map it mechanically to English simple/progressive or Estonian object case.
- Perfective verbs have no present-time meaning in their morphologically present forms; those forms normally refer to the future.

### Motion verbs

- Unprefixed motion verbs often contrast unidirectional/determinate with multidirectional/indeterminate movement.
- Prefixes add path and aspectual meanings, sometimes creating lexicalized senses.
- Select motion verbs from the actual path, mode, repetition, and viewpoint.

### Mood, voice, and agreement

- Present and future verbs agree in person and number.
- Past forms agree in gender and number.
- Form the conditional with a past form plus `бы`, whose position can vary with focus and clause structure.
- Imperatives vary by aspect, polarity, politeness, and pragmatic force.
- Russian passives, reflexive `-ся` forms, indefinite-personal clauses, and impersonal clauses overlap but are not interchangeable.

## Clause structure

- Neutral order is often subject–verb–object, but word order is guided strongly by topic, focus, contrast, rhythm, and genre.
- Russian normally has no present-tense copula equivalent to English `be` in simple nominal predicates.
- Do not insert pronouns simply because English requires an overt subject; Russian can omit contextually recoverable subjects.
- Negation can involve `не`, `ни`, negative pronouns or adverbs, and genitive constructions. Russian negative concord is grammatical.
- Use commas according to Russian clause and participial-phrase rules, not English intonation.
- Distinguish dash punctuation in zero-copula nominal predicates from a universal replacement for the missing verb.

## Usage and style

- Choose `ты` versus `вы` from relationship, number, setting, and user preference. Capitalized `Вы` is limited to particular direct-address conventions and is not required in all formal writing.
- Preserve the user’s gendered self-reference when known. If unknown and unavoidable, restructure neutrally or flag the choice instead of guessing.
- Avoid bureaucratic nominalizations and passive chains unless the genre requires them.
- Prefer established Russian terminology, but retain accepted international or technical terms when they are clearer.
- Distinguish normative correction from colloquial, professional, regional, or internet usage.
- Apply Russian conventions for dates, initials, abbreviations, spacing, dashes, and units in localized text.

## Transliteration

- Ask for or infer the governing standard when transliteration has official consequences.
- Distinguish reversible scholarly transliteration, library standards, passport or government systems, and reader-friendly romanization.
- Do not mix systems within one document.
- Transliteration converts script; transcription represents pronunciation. Label the requested output correctly.
- Preserve an already official Latin spelling unless the user explicitly requests conversion under a named standard.

## Frequent learner problems

- Choosing case by literal translation rather than government.
- Mismatching adjective, participle, or past-tense agreement.
- Treating aspect as completed versus incomplete in every context.
- Confusing motion-verb directionality and prefixes.
- Omitting negative concord.
- Overusing pronouns or the present copula under English influence.
- Using English word order without Russian topic–focus structure.
- Failing to mark stress in pronunciation-focused material.
- Mixing `ты`, `вы`, and `Вы`.
- Translating English articles as demonstratives unnecessarily.
